# The Agentic Coding Paradox: Why AI That Grinds Problems Into Dust Might Be Grinding Software Engineering Too

There's a new kind of developer emerging in 2026. They ship fast. Their pull requests close issues at inhuman speed. Their code works—mostly. But ask them *why* it works, ask them to trace the logic without the AI assistant, and you'll see something troubling: a blank stare, a fumble for the prompt history, a quiet admission that "the AI figured that part out."

Matt Webb recently observed something profound about agentic AI systems: they don't solve problems the way humans do. They grind them into dust through brute-force token burning—throwing computational power at a wall until something sticks. It's not elegance. It's persistence. And it works. But at what cost?

The question isn't whether AI can write code. It can. The question is whether a generation of developers who lean on these tools are learning to *think* like engineers—or just learning to *prompt* like users.

## The Shift From Craft to Completion

Software engineering used to be a craft of constraints. You had limited memory, limited processing power, limited time. Elegance wasn't a luxury—it was survival. You learned to think in systems, to trace dependencies, to understand *why* a solution worked because debugging required comprehension, not just trial and error.

Agentic AI changes the game. Tools like Cursor, Copilot Workspace, and Devin don't just autocomplete—they *auto-solve*. Give them a task, and they'll iterate, test, refactor, and ship. They'll burn through thousands of tokens, trying approaches a human would never consider because humans optimize for understanding, not just outcomes.

This is what Webb means by "grinding problems into dust." The AI doesn't need to understand the problem deeply. It just needs to find *a* solution that passes the tests. And because compute is cheap and getting cheaper, why not try a hundred approaches in parallel? Why not let the machine do what it does best—brute force with style?

The efficiency is seductive. Startups love it. Junior devs love it. Project managers love it. But engineers who came up in the era of deliberate design are starting to ask: are we building software—or are we building dependency on software that builds software?

## The Hidden Cost of Outsourced Comprehension

Here's the paradox: the better these tools get at solving problems, the less developers are forced to solve them themselves. And unlike learning to drive where you can switch off cruise control and take the wheel, in coding, the abstraction layer is invisible. You don't *see* the AI's decision tree. You don't trace its logic. You just see the output.

This creates a generation gap—not in age, but in mental models. Developers who learned to code before powerful AI assistants have an advantage: they know what's under the hood. They can debug when the AI fails, refactor when the solution is technically correct but architecturally fragile, and most importantly, they can *teach* newer developers the *why* behind the *what*.

But what happens when the teaching pipeline itself is disrupted? When the pathway to mastery is paved with AI-generated code that works but isn't understood? We get brittle systems built by people who can ship features but can't architect solutions. We get codebases that reflect the AI's pattern-matching logic—functional but inelegant, correct but unmaintainable.

There's a broader lesson here from Stanford's recent study on AI chatbots giving personal advice. The researchers found that people over-rely on AI recommendations even when those recommendations contradict expert human judgment. The AI's confidence—its fluent, authoritative tone—creates a false sense of reliability. The same dynamic applies to code.

When an AI agent completes your function, fixes your bug, and writes your tests, it does so with the same confidence whether the solution is optimal or merely adequate. And because it *works*, we ship it. But "works" is not the same as "right." "Works" is not the same as "maintainable." "Works" is not the same as "understood."

## The Generational Question

Surah Al-Fath 48:29 speaks of a generation marked by strength and mercy, rooted in faith and mutual support. It's a vision of people who *know* why they stand where they stand, who aren't just following commands but understand the mission.

In software engineering, we need the same. We need a generation that doesn't just use tools—but understands them. That doesn't just ship code—but comprehends systems. That doesn't optimize for short-term velocity at the cost of long-term mastery.

This isn't about rejecting AI. I use these tools daily. OrcaSMS was built with AI assistance. Nurflix, Nurcast, BayarZakat—all of them benefited from code generation. But the difference is intentionality. The tool augments—it doesn't replace—the thinking.

The risk is that we're raising a cohort who never had to grind through the hard problems manually, who never had to read the documentation cover to cover because Stack Overflow wasn't enough, who never had to refactor a mess they themselves created because they never created the mess—the AI did.

And when the AI fails—because it will—what then?

## So What?

If you're a junior developer, this is your moment of choice. You can ride the AI wave and ship fast, or you can use the AI as a sparring partner—letting it generate code, then tearing it apart, understanding it, improving it. The latter is harder. It's slower. But it's the path to mastery.

If you're a senior developer, this is your responsibility. Don't just review code for correctness—review for comprehension. Ask your team: "Do you understand this?" Not "Did the AI generate it?" but "Can you explain it?"

If you're building products, ask yourself: are we optimizing for speed or for sustainability? Because code that ships fast but breaks slow is a tax on the future.

The agentic coding revolution is here. But revolutions don't just disrupt—they demand decisions. Will we grind problems into dust and call it progress? Or will we build a generation of engineers who know how to think, not just how to prompt?

## Take Home Points

- **Agentic AI optimizes for completion, not comprehension**—it will find *a* solution, not necessarily the *right* one
- **Brute-force token burning works, but it's not the same as elegant problem-solving**—there's a difference between code that passes tests and code that's maintainable
- **Over-reliance on AI tools creates a hidden knowledge gap**—junior developers may ship fast but lack the mental models to debug, refactor, or architect
- **Mastery requires deliberate struggle**—use AI as a sparring partner, not a crutch; tear apart its solutions, understand them, improve them
- **Senior developers have a duty to teach the *why*, not just review the *what***—code review should include comprehension checks, not just correctness checks
- **This is a generational moment**—the decisions we make now about how we use these tools will shape the next decade of software engineering
