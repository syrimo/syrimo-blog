The phrase stopped me cold: "burn a trillion tokens to grind a problem to dust."

Matt Webb said it casually, almost throwingly, describing how agentic AI coding assistants work. They don't give up. They don't get tired. They just keep hammering at a problem—trying variations, rewriting code, running tests, iterating endlessly—until the problem surrenders. It's computational brute force dressed up as intelligence. And we're supposed to celebrate this as progress.

But what if we're celebrating the wrong thing? What if the very quality that makes agentic AI powerful—its inexhaustible patience—is also what makes it dangerous? Not in a sci-fi sense, but in a very human one: the cost to our planet, our craft, and our minds.

## The Promise Looks Perfect

The pitch is seductive. Agentic AI coding tools—systems like Devin, Claude with extended thinking, or GPT-4 in autonomous mode—promise to take the grunt work out of development. You describe what you want, and the AI grinds through the solution space. It tries ten approaches where you'd try two. It refactors, debugs, rewrites, tests, and iterates without complaint.

Simon Willison's recent experiments show this in action. He describes "vibe coding"—giving high-level direction to AI and watching it build SwiftUI interfaces through relentless iteration. The AI doesn't care if it gets the syntax wrong the first five times. It just keeps going. No frustration. No coffee breaks. No moment of stepping back and thinking, "Wait, am I even solving the right problem?"

This is where the paradox lives.

## The Hidden Burn

Every token an AI processes costs energy. Every iteration burns electricity. Every retry consumes computational resources that translate to carbon emissions. When we say "burn a trillion tokens," we're not speaking metaphorically. We're describing actual resource expenditure on a scale most developers never see.

A single complex coding task might involve thousands of API calls, each one lighting up servers in data centers cooled by industrial HVAC systems. The AI doesn't optimize for efficiency—it optimizes for success, no matter the cost. It's like hiring a worker who will solve your problem but insists on flying around the world first, just to make sure they've checked every option.

Simon Willison's SwiftUI experiments were delightful to read—but they also involved letting an AI rebuild interfaces multiple times until they worked. How many tokens did that consume? How many kilowatt-hours? We don't know, because we're not shown the meter. The cost is invisible, externalized, someone else's problem.

But it's not. Every computation has a footprint. And when we normalize "just let the AI grind it out," we're normalizing computational waste at planetary scale.

## The Craft We're Losing

There's a deeper cost, harder to measure but equally real: what happens to us as builders when we outsource the struggle?

Programming isn't just about getting code to work. It's about developing intuition—learning to see patterns, to smell bad architecture, to feel when you're going down the wrong path. This intuition comes from repetition, yes, but also from *frustration*. From hitting walls. From rewriting the same function three times before realizing you were solving the wrong problem.

When an AI grinds through those iterations for you, you lose the chance to build that muscle. You get the solution, but not the wisdom that comes from the search.

Simon describes the joy of "vibe coding"—sketching out ideas at high level and letting AI handle the details. It *is* joyful. But joy and growth aren't always the same thing. A student who uses calculators for everything might get the right answers, but they won't develop number sense. A writer who only edits AI-generated prose might produce content, but they won't develop voice.

This isn't Luddism. I use AI tools daily. Sutera Hijau Academy's ORCA platform is built on AI. But there's a difference between augmentation and abdication. Between using AI to amplify your capabilities and using it to replace your growth.

The question isn't whether AI can grind problems to dust. It's whether we should let it grind *every* problem to dust, especially the ones we need to struggle with to become better.

## The Optimization Trap

Here's where it gets philosophically slippery. Agentic AI optimizes for machines that never get frustrated. But frustration is a feature, not a bug. It's a signal. It tells you when you're working too hard on the wrong thing. It forces you to step back, reconsider, maybe talk to a colleague.

An AI doesn't have that circuit breaker. It will happily spend ten thousand tokens trying to fix a typo in your prompt because it doesn't know exhaustion. It doesn't know the feeling of, "You know what, maybe I should just start over."

We're building tools that mirror our ideal of infinite patience and persistence. But humans aren't meant to have infinite patience. We're meant to have *strategic* patience—knowing when to push through and when to pivot. The friction isn't inefficiency. It's wisdom.

When we celebrate AI that "never gives up," we're celebrating the removal of a mechanism that keeps human problem-solving adaptive and context-aware. We're optimizing for computational stamina while ignoring human judgment.

## So What?

This matters because we're at an inflection point. Agentic AI is moving from experiment to infrastructure. Companies are integrating these tools into core workflows. Developers—especially younger ones—are learning to code in an environment where the AI does the grinding.

If we don't wrestle with the paradox now, we'll wake up in a world where:
- Our computational carbon footprint has exploded while we congratulate ourselves on productivity
- A generation of developers can describe what they want but can't debug what breaks
- We've confused "getting things done" with "building things that matter"

The irony is sharp: we're using AI to save time and energy, while burning both at unprecedented scale. We're trading human frustration for planetary exhaustion.

This isn't about abandoning agentic AI. It's about using it *wisely*. With intention. With awareness of cost—environmental, educational, existential. It's about asking not just "Can AI solve this?" but "Should AI solve this? What am I giving up if it does?"

Because some problems *should* be ground to dust. But others need to be wrestled with, not outsourced. The wisdom is knowing which is which.

## Take Home Points

- **Computational brute force has real environmental costs** — every trillion tokens burned is energy consumed, carbon emitted. Invisible doesn't mean free.
- **Struggle builds competence** — frustration isn't inefficiency, it's feedback. Outsourcing all iteration means outsourcing all learning.
- **Infinite patience isn't always a virtue** — AI that never gives up lacks the strategic judgment to know when pushing harder is the wrong approach.
- **Augmentation ≠ abdication** — use AI to amplify your skills, not replace your growth. There's a difference between letting AI handle boilerplate and letting it handle everything.
- **Ask "should" before "can"** — just because agentic AI can grind any problem to dust doesn't mean every problem should be solved that way. Intention matters.
